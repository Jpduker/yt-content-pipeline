## Eight editorial gates

1. Open with a specific situation already going wrong. The first sentence should raise a clear question in roughly three seconds. Avoid scenery introductions and a long explanation of the job.
2. State what the protagonists want, and what failure costs, by the opening two shots. Money alone is not tension: establish the loss, rival, deadline, exposure, or damaged trust.
3. Make the obstacle resist the obvious solution. Check why they cannot simply drive away, break a flimsy barrier, carry a handful of cash instead of a crate, call, wait, or leave with both objects. Fix the story rather than hiding a practical hole with rapid narration.
4. Let Jason and Lucia make consequential choices. Their different instincts must affect the action; do not use one as a silent prop while the other explains everything.
5. Plant the detail that earns the reversal before the reveal. An insert, reaction or repeated sound should show it. Do not introduce an unseen tool, accomplice, payment, key or vehicle in the final seconds to rescue the plot.
6. Escalate through action and changed information. Every beat must change the problem. Avoid filler such as 'he finally understood' and generic menace that never affects a decision.
7. Resolve the central question with a visible consequence. A loose arc may continue, but an upload must deliver its own payoff. Avoid stopping just before the promised reveal.
8. Keep the batch varied: physical heist, pursuit, double-cross, dark comedy, trust conflict and risky escape. Avoid repeating a hidden tracker, a traitorous buyer, returning innocent people's cash, or abandoning the score in every episode.

## Pre-art review

Record goal, stakes, obstacle, planted clue, protagonist decision, payoff and causal check per story. Read the script aloud without pictures: is the situation interesting and clear? Then inspect each shot against the script. Reject or revise weak stories before spending narration credits or generating a large image batch.

The narration target is about 120–135 spoken words, with actual synthesized duration measured before editing. Keep the finished Short within 50–60 seconds without changing approved native pitch or forcing unnatural speed. Sentence lengths and shot durations follow the action, not a rigid equal-word-count template.

## Visual and sound continuity

Keep character identities, wardrobe, vehicle model/occupants, props, geography and chronology explicit. Establish where each important object came from, who holds it, and where it ends. Do not duplicate destroyed crates, return a departed suitcase, photograph blank evidence, or invent a new signature between shots.

Use specific score cues for pursuit, suspicion, confrontation, escape and payoff. Match rain, engines and sirens to physical perspective; keep the voice intelligible. Reusing an approved character reference is helpful; reusing an unrelated finished scene as new action is not.
